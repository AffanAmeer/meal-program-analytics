# Decision Memo: How Much to Cook and How Many Volunteers to Sign Up

**To:** Program Lead and Kitchen Coordinator  
**From:** Affan Ameer  
**Date:** October 2026  
**Based on:** 88 program days from 2023 to 2025 ([live dashboard](https://public.tableau.com/app/profile/affan.ameer/viz/MealProgramPlanning/Dashboard1))

## Recommendation

Cook a set amount for each kind of day:

- **Monday to Thursday:** about 810 meals, going up to 970 in the last 10 program days.
- **Friday to Sunday:** about 1,120 meals, going up to 1,310 in the last 10 program days.
- **Buffer:** start with 5% above a normal day. Use 8% if running short worries you more than waste.
- **Volunteers:** ask for 16 sign-ups Monday to Thursday and 24 Friday to Sunday.

Each target is the median number of meals served on past ordinary days of that kind, plus the buffer, rounded to the nearest 10. "Ordinary" leaves out days the food ran out and the unexplained slow days.

I tested this on 59 past days in 2024 and 2025. With the 5% buffer, waste would have dropped by 34%, and the kitchen wouldn't have run short any more often than it did. The volunteer rule would have cut short-staffed days from 9 to 6.

My suggestion is to start with 5% and try it for one program. If the team decides that never running short is worth some extra waste, switch to 8%.

## Why change anything?

Over three years the kitchen made 90,320 meals and threw out 9,709, about 11%. It also ran out of food on 7 nights.

Most of that waste was Friday to Sunday. Those days are 42% of the program but 72% of the waste. Even on a normal Friday to Sunday, the kitchen cooked about 170 to 190 more meals than people ate.

The 7 nights the food ran out tell the same story from the other side. On every one of them, the kitchen had cooked less than it usually did for that kind of day. Friday to Sunday cooking ranged anywhere from 970 to 1,370 meals.

So the kitchen is wasting food and running out for the same reason: the amount cooked jumps around more than the number of guests does. A steady target should help with both.

## What the back-test showed

I planned each test year using only the years before it, the same way the kitchen would plan for real. These results cover 59 days in 2024 and 2025.

| Plan | Meals wasted | Short days |
|---|---:|---|
| What the kitchen actually did | 6,698 | 5 |
| Target + 5% | 4,413 (34% less) | 5 |
| Target + 8% | 5,950 (11% less) | 1 |
| Target + 10% | 7,008 (5% more) | 0 |

The 5% rule would have saved 2,285 meals over the two years. The 8% rule would have saved 748 and run short less often. The 10% rule never ran short, but it wasted more than the kitchen does now.

Almost all of the saving comes from Friday to Sunday. There, the plan cooks about 85 to 130 fewer meals a day and cuts waste roughly in half. Monday to Thursday stays close to what the kitchen cooks today, just more steadily.

The result held up in both years: 35% less waste in 2024 and 33% less in 2025.

This is a good reason to try the targets, but it doesn't guarantee the same result next time. On the 5 past days the food ran out, the plan would have cooked 20 to 120 more meals than the kitchen did. Nobody counted how many people were turned away, though, so we can't say whether that would have been enough.

## 5% or 8%?

The data can't make this call for you. It comes down to what matters more to the program.

- **5%:** saves the most food, about 2,300 meals over two years. It still ran short on 5 days, by about 10 to 52 meals each time.
- **8%:** the safer choice. It saves less, but ran short only once in the test, by 12 meals.

If cutting waste matters most and the kitchen can make a few extra meals quickly on a busy night, I'd start with 5%. If running short is a serious problem for the people you serve, go with 8%.

## Volunteers

About 87% of people who sign up actually come, and that holds across years and days of the week.

The targets are 16 sign-ups Monday to Thursday and 24 Friday to Sunday. They come from a rule that plans for a worse-than-usual turnout, the kind that happens about 1 day in 10. In the test, that rule cut short-staffed days from 9 to 6, and on ordinary days from 3 to 1.

10 of the 13 short-staffed days were also the unexplained slow days below. That's worth looking into, but the records don't say what caused it.

This assumes the same share of people turn up when more people sign up.

## What this won't fix

**The slow days.** On 12 days, turnout dropped 25% to 30% below normal and about 300 meals were thrown out each time. Those days are 38% of all waste. The chef marked them "rainy", but 7 of the 12 had no rain at all, and days that actually were rainy weren't any quieter. Nothing in the data predicts these days.

**Weather.** Temperature and rain had almost no link to how many people came, so there's no reason to change the plan based on the forecast.

## Limitations

- It's only 88 program days. Some groups have few days, and the 2024 test learned from just 3 to 9 earlier days per group.
- On days the food ran out, nobody counted the people turned away, so real demand on those days is unknown.
- The volunteer test assumes the show-up rate stays the same when more people sign up.
- This is a test on past data. Only a real trial will show how the targets work in practice.

## Next steps

1. Use the cooking and volunteer targets for one program, then compare waste, shortages and staffing with these results.
2. Whenever the food runs out, count how many people are turned away. That shows how busy those nights really were.
3. Write down volunteer no-shows and when you find out about them, to see if they give an early warning of a slow night.
4. Ask the chef what the "rainy" notes meant, and look for whatever else might explain the slow days.

The goal isn't to predict every odd night. It's to cook more steadily, waste less, and collect better data for next time.
